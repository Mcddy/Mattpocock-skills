## 3. Derive the system

- **Governing rules:** one or two taken from the subject, such as a real proportion, a data unit, the actual content length or an action's duration, applied consistently. A rule earns its place by making a task or relationship clearer to the user. Keep the labels, units, scales and legends that help people read it; a rule that serves only a hidden rationale is decoration.
- **Hierarchy:** one element dominates, with real scale contrast. Decide what is small, quiet or absent. Reduce competing emphasis without deleting what a decision needs, and keep enough overview to compare; large isolated cards can hide it.
- **Type:** set for the actual words, with deliberate breaks, a fitting measure and tested fallbacks. Use display type where it earns attention and plain operational text elsewhere.
- **Colour:** drawn from the content and brand, as semantic tokens with contrast checked.
- **Density:** set by the task: scanning, reading, comparing or operating.
- **Tokens:** a compact set with defined roles, reusing any coherent existing scale.

Don't carry a house style to unrelated projects unless a shared style is scoped to them. A palette change is not a new direction.



## 4. Write before laying out

Draft the real copy first; layout follows content. The aim is to put the observation where the stock phrase was, not to make the voice shorter or harsher.

- **Name the point.** Write the one sentence the experience must deliver. The headline names the thing or the offer. Copy that narrates the interface means the structure isn't explaining itself.
- **Lead with the thing.** Open with the object, result or action, and name instances rather than categories.
- **Show or cut evaluation.** A word asserting quality ("seamless", "powerful") needs the consequence that makes it true, or it goes. A phrase announcing significance stands in for the observation, so state the observation. Domain terms used in their technical sense stay.
- **Give each string one job:** name, distinguish, predict an action, report state or prevent a wrong expectation.
- **Repeat with a purpose.** Repetition makes a claim feel truer without supporting it. Repeat only for orientation, learning or an independent context.
- **Recommend with reasons.** Give the reason and the real trade-off, with no guilt, false urgency or fake alternatives. Direct task instructions are fine.
- **Simplify without merging distinctions.** "May" stays "may"; two fees stay two fees.
- **Distinguish alternatives** by what makes someone choose one over the other. If two descriptions could swap and stay true, the distinction is missing.
- **Keep roles apart.** Word input, observed facts, computed results and suggestions so none passes for another.
- **Match promises to behaviour.** State material limits once, where they change a decision.
- **Write in a speaker's voice** with a stance fitted to the subject. When editing, keep the writer's voice. A triple, a "not X but Y", a rhetorical question or a one-line paragraph is fine when the content has that shape, and stock when it's there for rhythm. Don't overcorrect into clipped or combative copy, and don't cut context the reader needs.
- **Protect exact text:** quotations, code, identifiers, URLs, citations and required notices.
- **Follow conventions.** Use the user's spelling and punctuation; when a mark is ruled out, rewrite rather than substitute. Native locale formats are conventions, not faults.
- **Keep internal vocabulary internal.** Build and QA terms stay out of the product unless users need them.



## 5. Make distinguishing assets

- Prefer real assets, shown large enough for their detail to work and cropped with intent.
- When you must create imagery, diagrams or sample data, generate them from a rule tied to the subject, using a restricted vocabulary that stays consistent across the set. Inspect each at final size and at thumbnail.
- Depict stated processes and materials faithfully. Stylised wear or hand marks may serve an honest aesthetic; never use them to imply unobserved handmade provenance, real history or human authorship.
- Give sample data a realistic shape: real-length names, uneven counts, ranges, missing values and consistent totals. Label it as sample and recompute it after changes.
- Use stock imagery vocabulary (abstract blobs, isometric people, generic dashboards) only when the subject calls for it.
- Copy describes only what an asset actually contains. Make exact the two or three details that carry the character.



## 6. Build the behaviour

Model input, state, representation, computed output and consequence before adding effects.

- **States:** cover those that apply: default, selected, hover, focus, pressed, disabled, busy, empty, partial, invalid, error, success, unavailable and recovery, plus interruption, dismissal and focus return.
- **Controls:** every control changes something meaningful or is honestly unavailable. Headline, preview, status, price and output always describe the same state.
- **Inspectable choices:** show visual options beside the choice, make comparisons expose differences, and keep previews faithful or state their limits. Nonvisual choices get accurate consequences, not invented animation.
- **Narrow screens:** recompose around the next action and keep its result visible.

Prove one demanding slice first: real or labelled content, the primary action and a hard state, rendered at target sizes. Revise until it meets the brief, then extend. For a one-screen task, the slice is the deliverable.

Preserve routes, integration IDs, labels, data semantics and working actions unless the improvement requires changing them.



## 7. Motion and expressive mechanics

Each motion needs:

- a trigger;
- the relationship it explains;
- timing and interruption behaviour;
- an end state;
- a reduced-motion alternative that keeps state feedback.

Content is available immediately: no scroll hijacking and no hover-only essentials. Repeated actions get quiet, quick motion.

Expressive motion, sound, theme or game mechanics belong where they show something true, give feedback or motivate the actual task. They stay only if they hold up at full speed, when interrupted and with reduced motion. There is no quota either way. The available evidence, including historical reported context about plain and gamified versions of one app (see evidence.md), gives no basis for banning expressive design or for declaring minimalism best. When it matters, compare variants on the task.

Prefer transform and opacity, and measure the cost when performance is load-bearing.
