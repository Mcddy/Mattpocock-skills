## 4. Choose a direction from the subject

Look at the main object at the size users will see it. Note:

- what is distinctive about it;
- what users compare;
- the content's real shape: long names, ranges, empties, outliers;
- the context of use;
- what an expert checks first.

When the direction is open and the work is substantial, write three organising ideas, each from a different source:

- **The object:** the thing itself becomes the structure.
- **The decision:** the hardest judgement is made visible and operable.
- **The context:** the moment, device or pace of use shapes the design.

For each idea, give one sentence, what a viewer sees first, what changes in layout, copy and behaviour, and what it gives up. A palette or mood isn't an idea, and variants with the same structure count as one idea.

Choose the idea that eases the hard decision, fails the noun swap and can be built at quality within scope. Then write the line you'd want a stranger to say after five seconds. Skip this step when a direction is already chosen or the task is small.

Derive the system from the subject:

- one or two governing rules, such as a real proportion, a data unit, actual content length or an action's duration, applied consistently (a rule earns its place by making a task or relationship clearer to the user; labels, units, scales and legends that help people read it stay, while a rule that serves only a hidden rationale is decoration);
- one dominant element with real scale contrast, and deliberate choices about what is small, quiet or absent;
- a palette from content and brand, as semantic tokens with contrast checked;
- type set for the actual words, with tested fallbacks;
- density set by the task: scanning, reading, comparing or operating;
- a compact token set with defined roles, reusing any coherent existing scale.

Don't carry one product's treatment to unrelated products unless a shared style is scoped to them.

Make choices inspectable:

- visual options shown beside the choice;
- comparisons that expose differences;
- previews that are faithful or state their limits;
- nonvisual choices described by their accurate consequences.

On narrow screens, recompose around the next action rather than stacking desktop columns. Keep native controls and semantics, and put distinctiveness into composition, content, imagery and density.



## 5. Build one demanding slice

Before extending, build one representative screen with:

- real or labelled sample content;
- the primary action;
- a hard state, such as a long value, an empty result or a recoverable error.

Write its actual copy before styling, render it at target sizes and revise. A palette swap, or a screenshot with invented metrics, isn't proof.

Then carry the composition through the authorised scope. Preserve routes, integration IDs, labels, data semantics and working actions unless the improvement requires changing them. Don't invent customers, traction or capabilities.



## 6. Complete the experience

- **Copy:** read every visible string, across states, as one sequence. Headlines name the thing, actions predict their result and errors give a next step. Names stay identical from choice to preview to output.
- **Facts and roles:** a fact that changes the next action has one primary home, at body size or larger, beside that action. History and provenance can support it. Distinguish input, observation, computation and suggestion through structure, not colour alone.
- **Limits:** sample, fictional or prototype status and capability limits appear where they change a decision, not beside every control or buried in a footer. No automatic AI badges: follow the user's disclosure choice and never claim human authorship.
- **States:** default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, empty, partial, error and success, with feedback, recovery and undo where they matter. Captures lead with a populated working state; the product keeps purposeful empty states.
- **Access:** native or semantic markup, keyboard operation, accessible names, labelled inputs, sufficient contrast and target size, and reduced-motion support. No essential hover-only actions and no content gated behind animation.
- **Expressive elements:** motion, sound, theme and game mechanics stay when they explain state, give feedback or motivate the actual task, and they need a quiet fallback. Remove them when they delay or distract.
- **Internal vocabulary:** keep build and QA terms out of the interface unless users need them.
